# Step 60: Online widening of a referenced key

- **Phase:** Migrations
- **Depends on:** 52
- **Can run beside:** 08, 09, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48
- **Crates:** `morm-migrate`
- **PR approval:** CI and an independent review

Read `../rules.md`, `../contracts.md` and, once it exists, `../proofs.md` first. Everything needed for this step is in those files and this one.

## Goal

Change the type of a key that foreign keys reference, such as an `integer` primary key to `bigint`, or any other change within one type family that rewrites the table, on live tables with no long lock, together with every column that references it and its identity sequence.

## Deliverables

- The group op over the key, every referencing column and the identity or owned sequence, its steps with their boundaries, the shadow unique indexes and checks, the new foreign keys, the cutover, the sequence carried with its options and position, the refusals with their evidence, `--fast`'s refusal of an undeclared foreign key and `max_rewrite_mb` per table, the inverse for `rollback` and `revert` with its range check, and recovery at each boundary
- Every rewriting change within one family, the cast check with the collation named, the copy key for a table with no primary key, key values that change and the refusal of keys that would merge, the fit predicates and their CI table, the new refusals, and `--fast`'s drop and re-add of the group's foreign keys and its index rebuild

## End-to-end check

On Postgres 15–18, an `integer` identity primary key referenced by two tables widens to `bigint` under live readers and writers, cascading deletes included, with a cutover of milliseconds, no failed app transaction and no lost write, and ends on the catalog a fresh build gives. It goes back down online and up again, and the process killed at every step still reaches the right state. A `numeric(20,4)` primary key referenced by another table goes to `numeric(20,2)` online under `--phase contract`, with no failed app transaction and no orphan, and keys that would merge are refused before anything changes.

### Migrations / Online widening of a referenced key

**R-1121** **What changes together.** A type change on a column that a foreign key references, where the table has to be rewritten or the column's collation changes, and the old and new types are in the same one of the type families step 41's `check_schema` accepts, runs online by default in this form, in either direction. That's every such change within one family, not only between `smallint`, `integer` and `bigint`: an `integer` primary key becoming `bigint` or going back, an `integer` key becoming `numeric(20,0)`, a `numeric(20,4)` key becoming `numeric(20,2)`, a `varchar(10)` key becoming `varchar(3)`, or a `char(3)` key becoming `char(5)`. On Postgres 18.6 and 17.11, the table's file changed for a change of `numeric` scale, a lower `numeric` precision, `numeric(10,0)` to `bigint` or `numeric(8,0)`, `integer` to `bigint` or `numeric(12,0)`, a lower timestamp precision, a shorter `varchar`, `text` to `varchar(n)`, every change of a `char` length, longer included, and every array change, such as `int[]` to `bigint[]` or `varchar(5)[]` to `text[]`. It stayed the same for a higher `numeric` precision at the same scale or unbounded `numeric`, a higher timestamp precision, and a longer `varchar` or `text`. So a `char` or array key reaches this form even when it gets longer, and so does every narrowing that rewrites. The group is every column of the changed key, plus every column that references it, followed transitively: a referencing column that is itself referenced brings its own referencing columns in. The group also includes the identity or owned sequence, every index and constraint on those columns, and the views and materialized views over them, carried as step 39 carries them. A composite key changes only the columns whose type changes, and keeps its other columns in each index and foreign key. A self-referencing table gets a shadow column for the key and one for the referencing column, with one sync trigger. A referencing column whose type doesn't change, such as a `bigint` column referencing an `integer` key that becomes `bigint`, stays in the group with no shadow column, and step 5's new foreign key starts from that column itself. Step 06's typed foreign keys make the models change every referencing column with the key, so the diff shows the whole group, and the planner makes it one op. A widening that needs no rewrite, such as a longer `varchar` or a higher precision on `numeric(p,0)`, stays step 39's change in place, and a change across families, such as `integer` to `uuid`, or `timestamp` to `timestamptz`, keeps step 39's refusal on a live table, with `--fast`.

**R-1122** **The steps,** each with its own boundary, and each brief lock taken under `lock_timeout` with retries, as step 33 says:

1. `tx` per table, the key table first: add a shadow column for each changed column, with its new type and its collation named, as step 39 does, and one sync trigger, `BEFORE INSERT OR UPDATE`, that copies each old column into its shadow. `ADD COLUMN` takes `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` and `CREATE TRIGGER` takes `SHARE ROW EXCLUSIVE`, each for under a millisecond.
2. `batch` per table: copy by the table's copy key, as R-1148 says, up to its bound, the highest key read once that table's trigger has committed, as step 52's copy does, with the cursor in `_morm.steps`.
3. `no-tx`: `CREATE UNIQUE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` for each primary key or unique index that holds a changed column, and `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` for every other index that holds one, each with the shadow column in place of the old one, so a composite key keeps its other columns. Each new index is named `_morm_new_<name>`, from the real index's name through the 63-byte name function, so step 14 maps a unique violation on a shadow index to the key's field.
4. `tx`, then a separate `tx`: add a `CHECK ... NOT VALID` on each shadow column, then `VALIDATE` it. The check is `(new IS NOT DISTINCT FROM CAST(old AS <new type>))`, with `new IS NOT NULL AND` in front for a `NOT NULL` old column, and for a type with a collation the cast side names the new column's collation, as `CAST(old AS <new type>) COLLATE "<collation>"`. That's one rule for every change: `new = old` can never pass where the values change, as `numeric(20,4)` to `numeric(20,2)` showed when it failed `VALIDATE` on 200,000 rows, and doesn't exist between arrays of different types. Any other CHECK naming a changed column is added on its shadow column `NOT VALID` and validated the same way. `VALIDATE` takes `SHARE UPDATE EXCLUSIVE`, so writes carry on.
5. `tx`, then a separate `tx`, for each foreign key that references a changed column: lock the referenced table `SHARE ROW EXCLUSIVE`, then add the new foreign key on the shadow columns, or on a referencing column whose type doesn't change, `REFERENCES` the referenced table's shadow columns, with the same actions, `MATCH` type and deferrability, `NOT VALID`. Then `VALIDATE` it, which takes `SHARE UPDATE EXCLUSIVE` on the referencing table and `ROW SHARE` on the referenced one, so writes carry on.
6. `no-tx`: `ANALYZE` the shadow columns.
7. `tx` per materialized view above the group: build it beside the old one, as late as possible, as step 39 does.
8. `tx`: the cutover, as R-1123 says.

Each index and CHECK on a shadow column takes its text from Postgres, by the route steps 39 and 52 use. Step 4's check proves two things in one scan: every row was copied, and with the value the cast gives, which is the value the sync trigger's assignment gives every row it stores. The cast depends only on the value, so a key and its references change alike. It also lets Postgres skip the scan when the cutover sets `NOT NULL` or adds the primary key, which `CHECK (new = old)` alone doesn't: on Postgres 18.6 and 17.11, the cast form validated on a key table and a referencing table going from `numeric(20,4)` to `numeric(20,2)`, `SET NOT NULL` logged that existing constraints were sufficient to prove the column holds no nulls, and `ADD PRIMARY KEY USING INDEX` worked. Step 5 waits for every copy and for step 3, because a key that isn't copied yet would fail the new foreign key's checks on app writes. The new foreign key references the shadow columns' unique index before that index becomes the primary key, which Postgres allows, and the cutover turns that same index into the key, so the foreign key stays tied to it. Step 5 takes the referenced table's lock before the referencing table's, because the other order waited on app transactions for hundreds of attempts.

**R-1123** **The cutover** is one short transaction across every table in the group, and it gives way to live transactions under step 39's rule, with the same settings, the same `--terminate-blockers` and the same `--resume`. In order:

1. Take each carried view's lock with its own `DROP VIEW`, from the outside in, as step 39 does. Then lock the tables `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE`: the key table first, then the referencing tables in name order. Readers, and writers going from a parent to its children, lock the key table first, and under nonstop traffic a cutover that locked the referencing tables first got in on none of 176 attempts.
2. Walk the group again under the lock, reading `pg_constraint` for every foreign key that references a changed column. Anything new stops the cutover with nothing changed.
3. Take the sequence's lock with a same-value `ALTER SEQUENCE ... INCREMENT BY <current>`, because `LOCK TABLE` refuses a sequence, then read `last_value` and `is_called`.
4. Drop the sync triggers and the old foreign keys.
5. On each table: drop the identity, which drops its sequence, the old primary key or unique constraints and the other old indexes on the changed columns. Retire each old column as step 38 does, dropping its `NOT NULL` and default and renaming it to its retired name. Rename each shadow column to the real name, then `SET NOT NULL` where the old column had it, which the validated check proves with no scan, and set the old column's default. Re-attach the primary key with `ADD CONSTRAINT <name> PRIMARY KEY USING INDEX` and each unique constraint with `ADD CONSTRAINT <name> UNIQUE USING INDEX`, then carry the sequence as R-1124 says.
6. Drop the checks, and rename the new foreign keys and indexes to the real names. Move a `REPLICA IDENTITY USING INDEX` on a replaced index to its new index, because dropping the old one leaves the table with no replica identity index.
7. Recreate the carried views and swap the materialized views, as step 39 does, refresh functions, as step 37 does, copy column comments and grants, and write the step as `done` in `_morm.steps`, all in this same transaction.

It holds `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` on every table in the group, `SHARE ROW EXCLUSIVE` and then `ACCESS EXCLUSIVE` on the sequence, and each carried view's lock. Everything inside it is a catalog change, with no scan and no rewrite.

**R-1124** **The sequence.** An identity key keeps its sequence's name and position. Once the shadow column has the real name, the cutover runs `ADD GENERATED ALWAYS` or `BY DEFAULT AS IDENTITY (SEQUENCE NAME <old name>, <old options>)`, the same kind as before, with the old start, increment, cache and cycle, and with a minimum or maximum the old sequence set past its type's default. A minimum or maximum that was the old type's default becomes the new type's, so an `integer` identity widened to `bigint` can reach `bigint`'s maximum. Then `setval` sets the `last_value` and `is_called` read under the lock. The new sequence doesn't keep the old one's owner, grants or comment, so the cutover copies them. A session open across the cutover gets `55000` from `currval` and `lastval` on that sequence until its next `nextval`, because the sequence is new under the same name, and the README says so. A `serial` key, with an owned sequence and no identity, keeps the same sequence, with its OID and position: `ALTER SEQUENCE ... AS <new type>` changes its type, moving a maximum that was the old type's default to the new type's and keeping one set explicitly, and `OWNED BY` and `SET DEFAULT` move it to the new column. Postgres allows identity, and sequences, only on `smallint`, `integer` and `bigint`, so all of this holds within those three types, and R-1125 refuses an op that takes a generated key out of them while dropping its generation. A `serial` key's `ALTER SEQUENCE ... AS` runs only when its new type is one of the three. A `serial` key moved to `numeric(p,0)` with its `nextval` default kept keeps its sequence as it was, with `OWNED BY` and `SET DEFAULT` moved to the new column, and on Postgres 18.6 and 17.11 one did keep working, with its sequence still `integer`. Its position is checked as R-1127 says, because a `numeric(5,0)` key with a `nextval` default got `numeric field overflow` on the insert after its sequence passed 99,999.

**R-1125** **Refused online, with the evidence.** Each of these is refused with exit code `2` before the op's first step, changing nothing. `makemigrations` checks what scratch shows, `migrate` and `rollback` preflight check the live database, and the cutover's second walk checks again under its lock, where anything new stops it with nothing changed.

1. A foreign key on a changed column from a table no model declares, or one the models don't declare. The evidence is the referencing table, the constraint's name and `pg_get_constraintdef`, its deferrability and the table's estimated rows. morm can't change a column it doesn't manage, and if that column stayed narrow, its foreign key would fail once a value passed the old limit. It's a refusal, not a decision, because no answer would make the change safe. The fix is to declare the table in the models, or to drop that foreign key first. `--fast` refuses it too, as R-1126 says. The cutover's `DROP CONSTRAINT`, which never uses `CASCADE`, is the last check, because Postgres refuses to drop a key a foreign key needs, with `2BP01`.
2. A partitioned table or a partition in the group, because `ADD CONSTRAINT ... USING INDEX` isn't supported on a partitioned table, Postgres 15 to 17 can't add a `NOT VALID` foreign key from one, and Postgres can't add a column to a partition. `--fast` handles it.
3. A table in the group that's in any publication, or is a subscription's target, naming the publication or subscription, because the shadow columns and the cutover each stop replication with `missing replicated column`. `--fast` handles it, and the subscriber keeps working.
4. What step 52 refuses for an enum swap, on a column in the group: exclusion constraints and extended statistics, stored generated columns that read one, a domain or composite type, and a view or materialized view that would still read the retired column once recreated, such as `SELECT *`. Each of these needs a rewrite, or a definition morm can't move with no lock. `--fast` handles them.
5. Anything step 39 refuses above a swapped column, such as a view no model declares.
6. A table in the group that's in old-style inheritance, as a parent or as a child, naming the table and its parent or children, because `ADD COLUMN` on a parent adds the column to every child, `ALTER TABLE ONLY` can't stop that, and an inherited column's type can't change on the child alone. `--fast` handles it.
7. A table in the group with no primary key and no unique `NOT NULL` key, naming the table, because its copy has no key to bound it or resume it by, as R-1148 says. `--fast` handles it.
8. A referencing column whose new type is in a different btree operator family from the key's new type, naming both columns, such as a `bigint` column referencing a key that becomes `numeric(20,0)`. Postgres allows that foreign key, but compares through a cast, so it can't use the referencing column's index: deleting 10 keys took 683 ms on 18.6 and 668 ms on 17.11, with a parallel sequential scan of the referencing table filtered on `(pid)::numeric`, against 2 ms with matching types. `--fast` refuses it too.
9. When the change alters stored values, as R-1149 says, a referencing column that doesn't take exactly the key's new type, naming it, because a reference left at the old scale would no longer match its key. `--fast` refuses it too.
10. One op that takes a generated key, an identity column or a `serial` key with its owned sequence, out of `smallint`, `integer` and `bigint` while dropping its generation, naming the column. Postgres allows identity and sequences only on those types: on 18.6 and 17.11, `ALTER COLUMN ... TYPE numeric(20,0)` on an identity column, the `ADD GENERATED ... AS IDENTITY` this op's cutover would run on a `numeric` column, and `CREATE TABLE` with a `numeric` identity column each failed with `identity column type must be smallint, integer, or bigint`, and `CREATE SEQUENCE ... AS numeric` failed with `sequence type must be smallint, integer, or bigint`. So no sequence can count past `bigint`'s maximum, and a generated key gains no room as `numeric`. `--fast` refuses it too, because Postgres can't do it either. The way through is two migrations: the first drops the identity or the default, which changes no rows, and the second changes the type, with no sequence involved. `DROP IDENTITY` keeps the data and drops the sequence, and step 41 refuses at pool start a build that still leaves that column out of its inserts.
11. A collation change on a key with a nondeterministic collation on either side, naming both collations, because it can merge keys, as `'abc'` and `'ABC'` did when a key went from `"C"` to a case-insensitive collation, and Postgres refuses a foreign key between two different collations when either is nondeterministic. Between two deterministic collations, equality is byte equality, so nothing merges, and the change runs online. `--fast` handles it, as R-1126 says, and a merge there fails on the unique index and changes nothing.

An enum key keeps step 52's refusal, because an enum swap isn't a change within one family.

**R-1126** **`--fast` on a referenced key.** With `--fast`, a type change on a key that foreign keys reference changes every column in its group with `ALTER COLUMN ... TYPE` in one transaction, as step 39 does, naming each column's collation. It refuses a foreign key from a table no model declares, or one the models don't declare, with exit code `2` and R-1125's evidence, because Postgres would change the key and leave that column as it was, so its foreign key would fail once a value passed the old limit. It refuses R-1125's referencing column of another operator family, a referencing column that doesn't take the key's new type when values change, and a generated key leaving the integer types the same way. Postgres can't change the columns with the foreign keys in place when the change alters stored values, when the key's old and new types are in different btree operator families, as `integer` and `numeric(20,0)` are, or when the key's collation changes with a nondeterministic collation on either side. On Postgres 18.6 and 17.11, going from `numeric(20,4)` to `numeric(20,2)`, changing the referencing column first failed its foreign key against the old key, and changing the key first failed with `Key (pid)=(1.0051) is not present`, and for `integer` to `numeric(20,0)` the old foreign key `cannot be implemented`, because `numeric` and `integer` are in different operator families. So in those cases, in the one transaction, it drops the group's foreign keys, changes every column, then adds them back with the same names, actions, `MATCH` type and deferrability, validated under the lock it already holds, which worked in each case. `integer` to `bigint` needs none of this, and keeps them in place. After the change, it compares each index on the group's columns with scratch's definition and recreates any that differs, in the same transaction, because `ALTER COLUMN ... TYPE` silently swapped an explicit `int4_ops` for the new type's default class and kept `varchar_pattern_ops` on `text`, so the indexes could differ from the models with no error. An `ops` that doesn't fit the new type fails on scratch, naming the index and its `ops`, as `operator class int4_ops does not accept data type bigint`. A key change between `timestamp` and `timestamptz` converts every column in the group through the zone step 39's decision names. Step 51's `max_rewrite_mb` applies to each table in the group: preflight refuses with exit code `2` when any one of them is bigger, for `migrate` and `rollback`, naming that table and its size.

**R-1127** **The inverse, for `rollback` and `revert`,** runs this form in the other direction, online, and so does a migration that narrows a referenced key within one family. The retired columns become the shadow columns again: a sync trigger from the new columns to the old, a full copy, because rows changed since the cutover need it too, the indexes, checks and foreign keys, then a cutover that renames back and moves the identity back to a sequence of the old type. So the old columns come back at their original positions. Once `purge` has dropped them, it adds new shadow columns, as the up does. Preflight of `migrate` and `rollback` refuses with exit code `2` before the first step when any value in a changed column doesn't fit the narrower type, by R-1150's check, naming the table, the column, a value that doesn't fit and how many don't, or when the sequence's position doesn't: an identity's or a `serial` key's sequence against the narrower type's range, and a sequence behind a `nextval` default on a `numeric(p,s)` key against `10^(p-s)`. The cutover reads the sequence again under its lock and stops with nothing changed if its position no longer fits. Nothing can make that change safe once a value doesn't fit, so it's a refusal, not a decision. The copy step runs R-1150's check again once its sync trigger has committed, which catches a row written between preflight and the trigger, and stops with the same message, not a raw error mid-copy, and `rollback --failed` then drops the shadow objects. From the first step, an app write the narrower type can't hold gets the database's error from the sync trigger's assignment, such as `22003` for an identity value past `2^31 - 1`, or `22001` `value too long for type character varying(3)`, naming the shadow column's type. That's step 41's rule for a narrowing, the database's error for that write, applied from the first step rather than from the cutover, and after the cutover the column has the narrower type, so the database's error applies there too.

**R-1128** **Recovery.** Each step has a precondition, so `--resume` carries on from the boundary where it stopped. A rerun of steps 1 and 3 to 5 reads the catalog first and does only what's missing. A concurrent index left `indisvalid = false` is dropped and built again, a constraint left `NOT VALID` is validated again, and the copy resumes from its cursor. The cutover is one transaction, so a process killed inside it changes nothing, and a rerun starts it again. `rollback --failed` drops the shadow objects, which are the sync triggers, shadow columns, new indexes, checks and foreign keys, and leaves the old columns, keys and sequence in place.

**R-1148** **The copy's key.** Each table's copy walks by its primary key. A table with none is walked by a unique `NOT NULL` key: a unique constraint or unique index with no predicate and no expression, whose columns are all `NOT NULL`, the one with the fewest columns, then the first by name, with the cursor and the bound on it as on a primary key. A table with neither is refused online, as R-1125 says, because the copy would have nothing to bound it or resume it by, and `--fast` handles it.

**R-1149** **Key values that change.** A key change that's one of step 41's silent narrowings, a lower scale, a lower timestamp precision or a shorter length that drops trailing spaces, changes stored keys, such as `1.0051` to `1.01`, and runs in this form, held for `contract` as step 41 holds every silent narrowing, or under `--fast` as R-1126 says. A `char` key that gets shorter changes no value, because Postgres compares `char` without its padding. Step 4's cast check passes where `new = old` can't, and a key and its references round alike. Before the op's first step, `migrate` and `rollback` preflight refuse with exit code `2` any key values that would merge, naming them, such as `1.0051` and `1.0149`, which both become `1.01`, with one `GROUP BY` per key, built from the new type, in a read-only transaction with `statement_timeout` off. No answer can make two keys one, so it's a refusal, not a decision. On Postgres 18.6 and 17.11, a merge made `CREATE UNIQUE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` fail with `Key (id_new)=(1.01) is duplicated` and left the index invalid, `timestamp` to `timestamp(3)` merged `.123456` with `.123`, `varchar(10)` to `varchar(3)` merged `'abc  '` with `'abc'`, and `--fast` failed the same way. A merge written after preflight fails step 3's unique index build, and the step stops as `failed`. Every referencing column must take exactly the key's new type, as R-1125 says, and step 41 words the `lossy` decision and its live counts for a key. During the window, a write whose key collides with another only once rounded fails on the shadow unique index, and step 14 maps that index's name to the key's field, so the error reads as one on the key. In `contract`, apps are built for the narrower shape, and step 13 stops them writing extra decimals, so this is rare.

Measured on Postgres 18.6 and 17.11: a key table of 300,000 `numeric(20,4)` primary keys, each ending `.0051`, referenced by 400,000 `NOT NULL` rows with `ON DELETE CASCADE`, went to `numeric(20,2)` through steps 1 to 6 and the cutover, run by hand with the cast check and the key table locked first. 8 `pgbench` clients ran nonstop, writing two-decimal keys: inserting a parent and a child, deleting by key range with its cascade, updating a parent and then reading its children, moving a child to another parent, and a join. On 18.6, 89,098 transactions ran and none failed, the copies took 61 and 81 bounded batches, and the cutover got in on attempt 3, holding its locks for 8.3 ms. On 17.11, 74,116 ran and none failed, with the same batches, and the cutover got in on attempt 2 at 7.0 ms. On both, no row was orphaned, every parent's new key was its old key rounded, every child whose key differed from its retired one had moved to another parent after the cutover, and the tables ended `numeric(20,2)`, with `parent_pkey` and `child_pid_fkey` on the new columns and no trigger left. Each was a single short run with no fault injection, which T-0230 and T-0231 add.

**R-1150** **Checking that a value fits a narrower type.** R-1127's check covers every narrowing in this form that raises a real error on a value that doesn't fit, in any family, because the silent ones change values instead, as R-1149 says. A write's assignment cast is the authority, and `ALTER COLUMN ... TYPE`, `UPDATE ... SET` and a plpgsql trigger's `NEW.x := NEW.y` all use it. An explicit `CAST` isn't, because `CAST('abcd' AS varchar(3))` gives `'abc'` with no error, while assigning `'abcd'` to a `varchar(3)` fails with `22001`. So preflight builds a predicate from the old and new definitions as `format_type` prints them, as step 41's row count is built, with no SQL parser, and runs one `SELECT count(*), min(v)` per column where it holds, in a read-only transaction with `statement_timeout` off. A value `v` doesn't fit when:

| New type | Doesn't fit when |
|---|---|
| `numeric(p,s)`, from `numeric` | `v <> 'NaN' AND (abs(round(v, s)) >= 10^(p-s) OR v IN ('Infinity', '-Infinity'))` |
| `numeric(p,s)`, from an integer type | `abs(v) >= 10^(p-s)` |
| `smallint`, `integer` or `bigint`, from `numeric` | `v` is `NaN`, `Infinity` or `-Infinity`, or `round(v)` is outside the type's range |
| `smallint` or `integer`, from a wider integer type | `v` is outside the type's range |
| `varchar(n)` or `char(n)` | `length(rtrim(v, ' ')) > n` |
| a lower timestamp precision | never, because it rounds and never raises an error |
| an array of any of these | any element doesn't, through `unnest` |

On Postgres 18.6 and 17.11, these agreed with the assignment cast on all 40 value and type pairs tried: numeric overflow after rounding, such as `99.995` into `numeric(4,2)`, a negative scale, `NaN` and `Infinity`, `numeric` into `integer` and `bigint` at the edges, such as `2147483647.50`, `bigint` into `numeric(5,0)`, trailing spaces, tabs, newlines, non-breaking spaces and multibyte text into `varchar(3)` and `char(3)`, timestamps at the top of the range, and one- and two-dimensional arrays. Without its `NaN` guard the `numeric` predicate disagreed on `NaN` on 18.6, because Postgres sorts `NaN` above every number, while `NaN` fits `numeric(4,2)`, so a CI test pins every row against the assignment cast. Timestamp precision rounds even at the top of the range: `294276-12-31 23:59:59.999999` became `294277-01-01 00:00:00` with no error. The sync trigger's assignment and the copy are exact backstops, so a wrong predicate can only cause a stop that `rollback --failed` cleans up, never wrong data. `pg_input_is_valid` would be exact, but Postgres 15 doesn't have it, and a `pg_temp` function assigning each value reads row by row in one backend with no parallel scan, and needs the `TEMP` privilege.
